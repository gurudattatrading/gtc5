What is a torsion spring?

A torsion spring is a helical spring that stores and releases energy by twisting. It has a leg at each end of the coil. When one leg is turned relative to the other, the coil winds up and pushes back with a turning force called torque, then returns the legs to their free position when the load is released.

Torsion springs are used wherever something must rotate and come back: hinges and flaps, latches and locks, clips and clamps, pedal and lever returns, switches, and counterbalance and return mechanisms in machinery and vehicles.

How a torsion spring works

The load on a torsion spring is a force acting on a leg, and the torque it produces is T = F × r, where F is the force and r is the distance from the coil axis to the point where the force acts. A longer leg therefore gives a smaller force at the tip for the same torque. Torque rises in proportion to the angle the spring is turned, so the rate is stated as torque per degree or per turn. Single and double torsion springs

A single torsion spring has one coil and two legs. A double torsion spring has two coils wound in opposite hands, joined by a centre leg, with one leg at each outer end. Because the two coils work together, a double torsion spring gives about twice the torque of one coil and pushes evenly on both sides, which is why it is common on lids, flaps, doors and clamps.

Left-hand or right-hand wind

A torsion spring is wound either left-hand or right-hand. It should be loaded in the direction that winds the coil tighter, not the direction that opens it, because that is the direction the spring is designed to take. Say clearly which hand you need and which way the legs will turn in use — the wrong hand is one of the most common reasons a torsion spring does not fit.

Design points that matter

  • Support the coil on a rod. A torsion spring needs a rod or pin through the centre so it does not buckle sideways. The coil gets smaller as it winds up, so choose a rod clearly smaller than the free inside diameter.
  • Give torque at an angle. State the torque needed at a stated working angle, not only the dimensions.
  • Allow room between coils. The body gets longer as the spring winds, so leave a small gap between coils so they do not rub.
  • Mind the bends at the legs. Torsion springs usually fail where a leg leaves the coil, because the bend concentrates stress. A generous bend radius helps, and so does avoiding repeated loading near the maximum angle.
  • Do not over-turn. Turning beyond the designed angle leaves the spring permanently set.
  • Mention the environment. Outdoor, wet or chemical use points to stainless steel or a protective finish.

Materials and finishes

Torsion springs are made from spring steel wire such as music wire or hard drawn wire, or from stainless steel where corrosion resistance matters. Plated, painted or galvanised finishes are common for outdoor use. See our material comparison and finishes and coatings guide.

Torsion spring FAQs

What is the difference between a torsion spring and a compression spring?

A compression spring pushes back along its axis when squeezed. A torsion spring works by twisting: when its legs are turned about the coil axis it produces torque, so it is used for hinges, latches, pedals, clips and return mechanisms.

What is a double torsion spring?

A double torsion spring has two coils wound in opposite hands and joined by a centre leg, with a leg at each outer end. The two coils share the load, so it gives roughly twice the torque of one of its coils and a more balanced push, which suits lids, flaps, doors and clamps.

Why does the direction of wind matter?

A torsion spring should be loaded in the direction that winds the coil tighter, because that is the direction it is designed to take. Loading it the other way opens the coil, raises stress and shortens life. Always say whether you need a left-hand or right-hand wind and which way the legs will turn.

How is a torsion spring specified?

By wire diameter, outside or inside diameter, number of coils, leg length and shape, direction of wind, free angle between the legs, and the torque needed at a stated working angle. Our torsion spring calculator and torque formula guide help you work out the numbers.

Can you make a torsion spring from a sample or a broken spring?

Yes. Send the sample, a broken spring with all pieces, or clear photos with measurements, and we will identify the wire, coils, leg form and direction of wind and quote a matching spring.
