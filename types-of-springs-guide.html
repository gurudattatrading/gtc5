TL;DR

Compression springs push apart under load, extension springs pull together, and torsion springs twist around a pivot. Die and disc/Belleville springs are specialty compression variants for tooling and high-load-in-small-space needs.

For humid, washdown or outdoor use, choose Stainless Steel (ASTM A313). For general-purpose strength at low cost, Music Wire or Hard Drawn Wire works well. For shock loads or high temperature, use Chrome Vanadium.

Two springs that look identical can behave completely differently — always confirm wire diameter, coil diameter, free length and coil count before ordering, or use the free Spring Rate Calculator.

Fundamentals

The Main Types of Springs

Eight spring families cover almost every mechanical, industrial and household application.

Compression Springs
Open, spaced coils that resist a pushing load and shorten under force — the most common spring type across machinery, mattresses and valves.
Extension (Tension) Springs
Tightly wound, close-coiled springs with hooks or loops at each end that resist a pulling force and return to length once released.
Torsion Springs
Store energy through twisting around a pivot rather than linear movement — used in hinges, levers, clamps and clothespin-style actions.
Die Springs
Heavy-duty compression springs with a rectangular wire cross-section, colour-coded by load rating, built for stamping tools and presses.
Disc / Belleville Springs
Stacked conical washers that deliver a very high load in a very small axial space — common in bolted joints and clutch assemblies.
Wire Forms
Custom-bent wire components shaped to a specific drawing rather than a standard coil — clips, brackets, handles and retainers.
Circlips & Snap Rings
Retaining rings that seat into a groove to hold components in place on a shaft or inside a housing bore.
Custom & Non-Standard
Built to your drawing, a physical sample, or reverse-engineered from a worn part when nothing standard fits.

Avoid These

Common Mistakes When Choosing or Ordering a Spring

  • Assuming visually similar springs perform the same — a wire diameter difference of even 0.1mm changes spring rate noticeably.
  • Ignoring the operating environment — corrosion resistance matters as much as raw strength for outdoor or washdown use.
  • Not specifying working length or load — free length alone isn't enough information to match a spring's performance.
  • Skipping the spring rate calculation — verify with the free calculator before ordering, especially for replacements.
  • Mixing up winding direction on torsion springs — left-hand and right-hand wound torsion springs are not interchangeable.
  • Reusing a fatigued spring's dimensions — a worn spring may have taken a permanent "set" and no longer reflects the original free length.
Reference

Glossary of Spring Terms

Free Length
The overall length of a spring when no load is applied.
Solid Height
The length of a compression spring when all coils are fully compressed and touching.
Active Coils
The coils that flex and contribute to the spring rate, excluding closed or ground end coils.
Spring Rate (k)
The force required to compress or extend a spring by one unit of length: F = k × x.
Wire Diameter
The thickness of the wire used to wind the spring — the single biggest factor in spring rate.
Mean Coil Diameter
The diameter measured to the centre of the wire, midway between outer and inner diameter.
Pitch
The distance between corresponding points on adjacent coils.
Initial Tension
The force that holds an extension spring's coils together before any load is applied.
Shear Modulus (G)
A material property describing resistance to twisting deformation — used directly in spring rate calculations.
Deflection
The distance a spring compresses, extends or rotates under a given load.
Set / Set Removal
Permanent deformation after overloading; "set removal" is a manufacturing step that pre-compensates for it.
End Type
How the spring terminates — closed & ground, open, hooked, or with formed legs on a torsion spring.

FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

What's the difference between a compression spring and an extension spring?

A compression spring resists a pushing force and shortens under load, with open, spaced coils. An extension spring resists a pulling force and stretches under load, with tightly wound coils and hooks or loops at each end.

How do I know if I need a torsion spring?

If the application involves rotation around a pivot point — a hinge, lever, clamp or clothespin-style action — rather than a straight-line push or pull, a torsion spring is almost always the right choice.

Which material is best for outdoor or washdown use?

Stainless Steel (ASTM A313) is the standard choice for outdoor, washdown, marine, food or pharma environments, since it resists corrosion far better than mild steel, hard-drawn wire or even chrome vanadium.

Can two springs that look identical have different spring rates?

Yes. A difference of even 0.1mm in wire diameter, or one extra active coil, changes the spring rate noticeably even if the springs look the same size at a glance.

Do you manufacture custom or non-standard springs?

Yes. We build to a drawing, a physical sample, or a reverse-engineered worn part, in addition to the standard compression, extension, torsion, die and disc spring ranges.
